PRD: QR Ordering & Back-Office Web App for
Local Restaurants
Version: 0.1 (Draft) | Date: 15 Oct 2026 | Status: For review
1. Overview
A web-based platform (no app install) for small local businesses such as restaurants,
cafés and food stalls. Customers scan a QR code at the table or counter, browse the
menu, place an order and add notes. The counterperson receives and processes orders in
real time. The owner gets sales records, profit and loss insight and daily operational
checklists.
The product has three interfaces: Customer, Counterperson and Admin (Owner).
2. Problem Statement
Small restaurants typically run on paper slips, verbal orders and a basic billing machine.
This causes:
Missed or wrongly taken orders, especially during rush hours.
Queues at the counter and staff time wasted on order taking.
No clear daily picture of sales, expenses and real profit.
Cash crunches at month-end because aggregator settlements are delayed while
salaries, rent and EMIs are fixed.
Manual, error-prone cash reconciliation and payroll calculation.
Expensive existing POS systems that are built for larger chains.
3. Goals and Non-Goals
Goals (Phase 1)
1. Let customers order from their phone via QR without installing anything.
2. Give the counter a live order queue with simple status control.
3. Give the owner daily sales visibility and a basic profit and loss view.
4. Let the owner customize the customer landing page without a developer.

4. Target Users and Personas
5. Scope: Phase 1 Features
5.1 Customer Interface (opened via QR)
FR-C1 QR landing. Each QR code opens the business's menu page. QR can be per table
(table number embedded) or per counter.
FR-C2 Menu browsing. Categories, item name, photo (optional), price, veg/non-veg tag,
availability status. Items marked unavailable are greyed out.
FR-C3 Cart. Select item, set quantity, add or remove items, see running total.
FR-C4 Notes. Per-item note and an overall order note (for example less spicy, no onion).
FR-C5 Order placement. Customer enters name or table number (phone number
optional) and confirms the order. An order ID is shown.
FR-C6 Order status. Customer sees live status: Received, Accepted, Ready, Done.

5.4 Daily and Monthly Checklists (Operational Module)
These are guided checklists with data entry fields. Phase 1 covers a lightweight version;
automation grows in later phases.
Daily: Cash and Sales
System shows expected totals by payment mode (cash, card, UPI) from recorded
orders.
Counterperson enters actual counted cash and actual card and UPI settlements.
System highlights the difference per mode and requires a short reason if the gap
exceeds a configurable threshold.
Auto-generated daily sales report (PDF or screen) is saved for the owner.
Daily and Monthly: Profit and Loss and Cash Flow
Compile sales and manually entered expenses into a P&L statement (daily, monthly).
Track pending settlements from online aggregators as a separate line, so the owner
sees receivable money vs. cash in hand.
Cash vs. profit check: show net profit alongside actual cash position and upcoming
fixed obligations (salary, rent, EMI). Alert when cash in hand plus expected
settlements is below upcoming obligations.
Bank statement reconciliation: Phase 1 is manual tick-off; CSV import is Phase 2.
Monthly: Payroll
Staff list with monthly wage, attendance (manual daily mark-in, mark-out).
Auto-calculate gross pay, deductions (PF, ESI, TDS where applicable) and net pay.
Reminder to have salaries ready before the 1st of the next month.
Payslip export per employee.
Note: statutory rates and thresholds must be configurable and verified with an
accountant before launch, because rules change.

Order flow: Customer scans QR → sees menu → adds items, quantity, notes → confirms →
order appears at counter with alert → counterperson taps Accept → Ready → Done →
customer sees status update at each step.

7. Non-Functional Requirements
Performance: Customer page loads in under 3 seconds on a 4G connection. Design
mobile-first and keep the page lightweight.
Real-time: New order reaches the counter within 2 seconds (WebSockets or server-
sent events with polling fallback).
Reliability: Counter interface should tolerate short internet drops (queue and retry,
clear offline banner).
Security: Role-based access, hashed passwords, HTTPS only, per-business data
isolation.
Data privacy: Collect minimum customer data. Comply with India's DPDP Act where
applicable.
Accessibility and language: Large tap targets; support English plus at least one
regional language (Hindi in Phase 1) for the customer and counter views.
Backup: Daily automated backup of order and financial data.
8. Suggested Tech Approach
Frontend: React or Next.js as a PWA (installable on the counter tablet or phone).
Backend: Node.js (or Django) with REST APIs and WebSockets.
Database: PostgreSQL with a business ID on every table (multi-tenant).
Notifications: In-app sound alerts, Web Push, optional WhatsApp or SMS later.
Hosting: Managed cloud with a low-cost starting tier.
QR: Generated per business and per table; encodes a URL like
app.com/b/<business>/t/<table> .
9. Data Model (High Level)
Business, User (role), MenuCategory, MenuItem, Offer, Order, OrderItem, Payment,
Expense, SettlementEntry, Employee, AttendanceLog, PayrollRecord,

10. Success Metrics
11. Phased Roadmap
Phase 1 (MVP, about 10 to 12 weeks): Sections 5.1 to 5.3 plus a lightweight closing
checklist and manual-entry P&L. Pilot with 3 to 5 restaurants.
Phase 2: Online payments (UPI gateway), automated payroll, bank CSV import,
WhatsApp order alerts, kitchen display screen, printer integration.
Phase 3: Inventory and wastage, loyalty and repeat-customer offers, aggregator
reconciliation, multi-branch, accounting export (Tally, Zoho).
12. Risks and Dependencies
Staff must adopt the counter screen; if they ignore it, the whole flow breaks.
Weak internet at the outlet.
Payroll and tax rules are sensitive; errors have legal consequences.
Payment gateway onboarding and fees.
Competition from free or cheap incumbents.

14. Why This Will Work for Small Businesses
1. Zero install for customers. A QR and a browser is a very low barrier; people already
use UPI and QR codes daily.
2. Saves labor. One counter person can handle more orders, since customers type their
own orders. This matters when staff is only two to five people.
3. Fewer order mistakes. Written notes (less spicy, no onion) travel exactly as typed
instead of by shouting.
4. Visibility the owner does not have today. Most small owners guess their profit. A daily
sales and P&L graph is a real upgrade.
5. Addresses a real pain point. The cash-versus-profit check and the pending settlement
tracker speak directly to month-end cash crunches caused by aggregator delays and
fixed costs.
6. Upselling built in. Suggestables and offers increase average order value with no extra
effort.
7. Cheaper than traditional POS. Runs on hardware they already own (a phone or tablet);
no heavy setup.
8. Customizable branding. Even a tiny café can look professional, which builds trust.
9. Growth path. Starting with ordering, then adding payroll and reconciliation, makes the
product a daily habit rather than a one-off tool.
10. Simple to pilot. Works at a single outlet with no integration into other systems.
15. Why This May Not Work for Small Businesses
1. Low digital comfort. Owners and staff may find a multi-feature dashboard
intimidating. Too many features (payroll, P&L) in the first version can reduce adoption.
2. Staff resistance. The counterperson already has a working routine. If an alert is missed
once or twice, they revert to verbal orders and the system loses trust.

3. Customer behavior. Many customers, especially older people or those in low-end
smartphone or low-data situations, prefer to just tell the waiter. QR ordering suits
cafés and food courts better than a family dhaba.
4. Unreliable internet. A dropped connection at the counter means missed orders, which
is worse than paper.
5. Data entry burden. P&L and payroll are only as accurate as the data entered. Small
owners rarely track expenses daily; manual entry may be skipped, making the graphs
misleading.
6. Compliance risk. PF, ESI and TDS rules depend on employee count, wage thresholds
and state. Wrong calculations can create legal trouble. Many small restaurants also
keep cash or informal payroll, which the app cannot fix.
7. Existing competition. Free or low-cost tools (Petpooja, Zomato and Swiggy partner
tools, PhonePe or Paytm business apps, free QR menu makers) already cover parts of
this. You need a clear difference.
8. Price sensitivity. Small businesses resist monthly fees. If the value is not obvious
within the first week, they cancel.
9. Aggregator dependence. Reconciling aggregator payouts well needs data from those
platforms, which are not open. Without integrations, this stays a manual process.
10. Support burden. Selling to many tiny businesses means constant onboarding and
phone support, which can cost more than the subscription brings in.
11. Scope risk for a small team. Three interfaces, notifications, graphs, checklists and
payroll is a lot to build well.
16. Recommendations to Improve Chances of Success
Start narrow: Ship QR ordering and the counter order stack first; they are the daily-
use core. Add the P&L graph and checklists after the first pilot proves usage.
Make the counter screen idiot-proof: large buttons, loud sound alert, and a fallback
reminder if an order sits unaccepted for 2 minutes.
Support pay-at-counter as the default so the flow does not depend on payment
integrations.
Pilot with 3 to 5 friendly outlets (cafés, fast-food counters, juice and snack shops),
observe them in person and iterate.
Reduce data entry: one-tap expense categories, recurring expenses (rent, salary) set
once, and a daily reminder.
